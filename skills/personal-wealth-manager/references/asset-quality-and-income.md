# Asset quality and income-producing assets

## Separate asset, income, and cash

A financial or owned income-producing asset is a legally or economically controlled claim that can produce future cash flow, appreciation, resilience, or financial choice.

Income is the cash flow produced during a period.

Cash is the liquid result, not proof that the underlying engine is durable.

Examples:

- a deposit is principal; credited interest is income;
- a rental property or legally owned royalty right is an asset; net receipts are income;
- cash already received is liquidity;
- principal withdrawals and unrealized gains are not sleep income.

Keep tax status separate. “Sleep income” does not automatically mean after-tax income.

## Income classes

### Active income

Requires roughly proportional current labor. Record it as cash flow for surplus calculations, but route career or capability development to `life-asset-planner`.

### Semi-active income

Needs continuing operations but does not require strictly proportional labor. Record only verified net cash and residual labor requirements; do not design the underlying business or operating capability here.

### Sleep income

Can continue for a period without proportional new hours, such as interest, dividends, net rent, or royalties from an already owned right.

Measure sleep income conservatively. Count recurring net cash after direct costs, platform fees, maintenance, depreciation, expected tax, vacancy or failure allowances, and the labor still required to keep the stream operating. Do not count unrealized gains, principal withdrawals, return-of-capital distributions, unreceived subsidies, private-loan principal, or hoped-for market returns as sleep income.

### Occasional income

Irregular one-offs: resale gains, gifts, temporary projects, subsidies not yet contracted.

### Candidate income

An idea or experiment with no repeatable evidence yet. Do not count it in reliable coverage.

For every income stream, record gross cash, direct costs, platform fees, tax status, depreciation, maintenance time, concentration risk, and net cash.

## Unified quality criteria

Score qualitatively; do not turn the score into false precision.

1. **Understandability** — can the user explain how value or cash flow is produced?
2. **Role clarity** — does it protect liquidity, diversify, grow, or produce income?
3. **Economics** — is expected net cash flow or long-term appreciation reasonable after all costs?
4. **Durability** — can it survive cycles, competitors, or platform changes?
5. **Control and rights** — what does the user actually own or control?
6. **Transparency** — are fees, risks, lockups, and dependencies visible?
7. **Maintenance fit** — does it consume acceptable money, attention, and time?
8. **Portfolio fit** — does its liquidity, term, currency, and risk match the user's plan?

A quality asset need not satisfy every criterion. It must have a clear role and no hidden fatal flaw.

## Bad or negative-compounding patterns

- high-cost debt or recurring penalties;
- assets with negative cash flow and no credible path to improve;
- products the user cannot explain and holds only because of promotion or authority;
- duplicated funds that add complexity without meaningful diversification;
- high fees, opaque rules, or liquidity mismatched to upcoming needs;
- concentration created by uncontrolled averaging;
- holding only to “get back to cost”;
- using historical profits as permission to lose more;
- an income claim with one critical counterparty, platform, or legal dependency and no backup;
- undocumented or repeatedly extended private loans, especially when repayment depends only on relationship pressure;
- concentrated lending to one borrower, lending from emergency or earmarked funds, or treating promised repayment as current liquidity.

“Bad” means harmful relative to the user's goal and constraints, not morally bad or currently down in price.

## Private loans receivable

Evaluate money lent to a private counterparty as a credit claim, not as cash or a sleep-income asset merely because interest was promised.

Check:

- documentary evidence;
- outstanding principal versus amounts already repaid;
- agreed due date and whether it has passed;
- actual repayment behavior, not only verbal assurances;
- interest, fees, collateral, guarantor, and enforceability that require professional verification;
- borrower concentration and relationship pressure;
- whether the user would make the same loan today;
- whether repayment is being counted toward a dated family payment.

Classify the receivable provisionally as:

- **performing/maintenance** — documented and repaying as agreed, but still excluded from liquidity;
- **watch** — incomplete evidence, unclear date, extension, or concentration needs resolution;
- **collection/exit candidate** — overdue, disputed, repeatedly extended, or no credible repayment path;
- **possible impairment** — recovery is doubtful; show face value and uncertainty separately, without inventing a write-down.

Do not give legal collection, tax, or enforceability conclusions without current official verification or qualified professional review. Prefer a small next action such as reconciling transfers, obtaining a written balance confirmation, or agreeing a dated repayment schedule. Do not advise new lending to recover an old loan.

## Asset decision labels

- **Quality** — keep or grow within an explicit cap.
- **Maintenance** — useful but should not consume more capital or time.
- **Candidate** — small experiment with a hypothesis, budget, and deadline.
- **Bad/negative-compounding** — reduce, redesign, or exit when practical.

For financial investments also use core, research, observe/freeze, and exit candidate.

## Sleep-income coverage and the Gap objective

Treat financial capacity for a planned Gap as a funding question, not as permission to maximize yield or stop working prematurely. The purpose and non-financial design of the Gap belong in `life-asset-planner`.

Calculate separate coverage ratios:

`personal sleep-income coverage = conservative recurring net sleep income / necessary personal spending`

`household sleep-income coverage = conservative recurring net sleep income authorized for household use / necessary household spending`

Do not mix the two. Report the period, currency, data freshness, and whether each stream is contractual, historically repeatable, variable, or only a candidate. Use a trailing period and a conservative forward estimate when possible; do not annualize one unusually strong month.

Define success in stages:

1. partial personal coverage;
2. full necessary personal-spending coverage;
3. full agreed household necessary-spending coverage;
4. resilient coverage with diversified income sources, maintenance capacity, and a margin for inflation, tax, vacancy, platform failure, and market stress.

A planned **Gap** is a user-defined bounded period with reduced or no labor income. This Skill assesses only whether its spending and contingencies are financially funded.

Before declaring Gap readiness, establish:

- the intended duration and personal versus household spending responsibility;
- a separately protected Gap spending bucket plus emergency and planned-expense reserves;
- which sleep-income streams are genuinely available during the Gap;
- insurance, debt, dependant, and large-payment constraints that affect financial readiness;
- a stress case in which sleep income falls and risk assets decline simultaneously;
- review, extension, and return-to-income triggers.

Funding a Gap partly from a deliberately accumulated spending bucket can be valid, but label it principal consumption rather than sleep income. Do not require permanent financial independence before permitting a finite Gap; require that the chosen duration is funded without relying on forced asset sales, unreceived income, or new debt.
