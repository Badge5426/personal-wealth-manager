# Cash flow and risk budget

## Separate three kinds of risk limit

### Objective capacity

How much loss or illiquidity the financial system can absorb without disrupting essential life:

- reliable income and its concentration;
- necessary expenses;
- dependants and obligations;
- liabilities;
- private loans receivable, their due dates, evidence, and repayment performance;
- liquid runway;
- known large payments;
- insurance or social-protection gaps;
- recovery capacity from future surplus.

### Subjective tolerance

How much volatility the user can experience without losing sleep, panic selling, relationship damage, or abandoning the plan.

Use observed behavior during prior drawdowns, not only a questionnaire.

### Household consent

How much jointly relevant risk the affected people explicitly accept.

Use the lowest binding limit. A financially affordable risk can still be behaviorally or relationally unacceptable.

## Cash-flow definitions

- **Reliable income**: recurring income with evidence and a conservative haircut for variability.
- **Sleep income**: net recurring cash flow not requiring proportional current labor.
- **Necessary expenses**: spending required to preserve housing, food, care, health, work, basic transport, and contractual duties.
- **Irregular necessary expenses**: annual insurance, repairs, gifts or family duties that predictably recur, education, and planned large payments.
- **True monthly surplus**: reliable net income minus necessary expenses minus monthly provision for irregular needs.
- **Actual monthly surplus**: reliable net income minus all actual recurring expenses and irregular provisions.
- **Investable monthly surplus**: the portion of actual surplus the user explicitly permits for long-term investing.
- **Liquid runway**: unearmarked, runway-eligible safe liquid assets divided by monthly necessary expenses.

Do not count uncertain subsidies, expected bonuses, projected viral growth, or unsold assets as reliable cash.

For variable income, start with trailing 12-month realized net cash after direct costs. Show the monthly average, median, lowest relevant run of months, and time required. Select and explain a conservative planning value; do not silently apply an arbitrary percentage.

## Useful ratios

`income coverage = reliable monthly income / necessary monthly expenses`

`sleep-income coverage = net monthly sleep income / necessary monthly expenses`

`actual surplus rate = actual monthly surplus / reliable monthly income`

`risk-asset ratio (financial) = risk assets / financial assets`

`risk-asset ratio (net worth) = risk assets / net worth`

`risk-asset ratio (authorized pool) = risk assets in a decision pool / investable assets authorized for that pool`

`recovery months = stress loss / investable monthly surplus`

If surplus is zero or negative, recovery months are undefined; report that directly.

Property and vehicles can make net worth look safe while providing little liquidity. Always show financial-asset and net-worth ratios together when non-financial assets are large.

A tradable stock or fund may be market-liquid but is not automatically runway-eligible. Earmarked cash and known unfunded payments must be removed before calculating runway or investable money.

Private loans receivable are not runway-eligible or investable cash before repayment. Show face value separately from any provisional recoverable value. If repayment timing or recoverability is uncertain, use “unknown” instead of an assumed haircut. Stress delayed or failed repayment when the user depends on that money for a dated obligation.

## Stress testing

At minimum show:

- risk assets down 10%, 20%, and 30%;
- concentrated sector or thematic positions down 40% and 50%;
- reliable income interrupted for a user-relevant period;
- known large payment arriving early;
- combined scenario when the same economic shock may hit income and investments.
- delayed or failed repayment from a material private borrower, especially when one borrower represents most receivables.

Basic calculation:

`potential loss = exposed amount × stress drawdown`

For a multi-asset portfolio, use asset-specific shocks when known. Do not claim correlation precision without data.

Translate loss into:

- currency amount;
- percentage of financial assets;
- percentage of net worth;
- months or years of reliable surplus needed to recover;
- whether the family comfort line is breached.

## Four risk limits

1. **Normal fluctuation budget** — ordinary volatility that requires no action.
2. **Hard portfolio loss cap** — loss that requires reducing new risk and reviewing the plan.
3. **Extreme red line** — loss or liquidity event that must not threaten essential life.
4. **Family comfort line** — maximum jointly acceptable exposure or loss.

State whether each limit is a loss amount, exposure amount, or drawdown percentage. These are not interchangeable.

Also define:

- the decision pool and accounts covered;
- whether it means current-to-trough stress loss, loss from contributed capital, or drawdown from a high-water mark;
- whether existing unrealized loss is included;
- the relevant time horizon.

A statement such as “I can lose 100,000” is incomplete until these fields are clear.

## Convert a loss cap to exposure

If the user can accept a maximum stress loss `L` and the chosen stress drawdown is `D`:

`maximum exposed amount = L / D`

Example: a 100,000 loss cap under a 40% sector shock supports at most 250,000 of that exposure before other risks and diversification effects. This is arithmetic, not a recommended position.

## Dynamic risk budget

Increase the risk budget only after evidence:

- realized and retained surplus over a meaningful period, such as trailing 12 months;
- liquid runway remains intact;
- no near-term payment is being borrowed from;
- prior drawdowns were handled according to plan;
- household consent is updated when relevant.

Do not increase it because of:

- unrealized profits;
- “house money” or “free chips”;
- one good month;
- projected side income;
- expected but not yet received capital, including a maturing deposit, property or business sale, inheritance, compensation, subsidy, or loan repayment;
- confidence after a price rise;
- the desire to recover a loss.

After expected capital is actually received, do not add it automatically to the existing risk pool. First fund known payments and the liquid runway, identify ownership and the decision pool, obtain required household consent, and then set a new risk budget prospectively. Never use a later inflow to rewrite an earlier cap breach as compliant.

## Behavioral red flags

- adding because the position is down without revisiting the thesis;
- position size growing beyond the original cap;
- refusing to sell only because the price is below cost;
- trying to trade frequently to repair an underwriting error;
- calling household safety money “temporarily investable”;
- mixing emergency funds with sector bets;
- using expected income to justify present risk;
- reporting only percentages when the cash loss would upset the user.

Turn each red flag into a precommitment rule.

## Allocation order

1. earmark known payments;
2. protect a sufficient liquid runway;
3. address expensive debt and forced-sale risks;
4. define the stable/defensive bucket;
5. set the core growth budget;
6. cap research and concentration risk;
7. define contribution, income-reinvestment, rebalancing, and review rules.
